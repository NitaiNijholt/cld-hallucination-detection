\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:problem_setup}
%==============================================================================

This section defines the task, hallucination types, and evaluation metrics used throughout the experiments.

\subsection{Task: CLD Generation}
\label{sec:task_cld_generation}

The target task is automated construction of Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs)---directed graphs where nodes represent system variables and edges represent causal relationships with polarity (\texttt{POSITIVE}, \texttt{NEGATIVE}, or \texttt{NONE}) and mechanistic explanations. Given a domain specification (target phenomenon, spatial/temporal scale, scope), the system generates a complete CLD capturing the causal structure underlying the specified system.

\subsection{Hallucination Definition}
\label{sec:hallucination_definition}

\subsubsection{Manifestations}
Hallucinations = \textbf{incorrectness in causal links}: erroneous \textit{absence} (FN) or \textit{presence} (FP) of links, wrong direction, or wrong causal narrative.

\subsubsection{Operationalization}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{GT Lit:} CLD from peer-reviewed literature. \textbf{Hallucination = FP/FN} w.r.t. literature CLD.
    \item \textbf{GT Synth:} LLM-corrupted edges. \textbf{Hallucination = corrupted edges} (corrupted=True).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Corruption Process (HaluEval-inspired~\cite{li2023halueval})}
The Corruptor introduces controlled errors to test judge detection (see Appendix~\ref{alg:corruption} for algorithmic details). Three corruption types with \textbf{rate=0.3}, balanced distribution:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Motivation corruption:} Replaces valid explanations with plausible-but-flawed reasoning
    \item \textbf{Spurious edges:} Converts \texttt{NONE} $\rightarrow$ false positive links (\texttt{POSITIVE}/\texttt{NEGATIVE})
    \item \textbf{Polarity flips:} Inverts \texttt{POSITIVE} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{NEGATIVE}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Key Parameters (Corruptor):} \texttt{model=gpt-4.1}, \texttt{temp=0.7}, \texttt{top\_p=1}, \texttt{rate=0.3}

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:system_architecture}
%==============================================================================

This section documents all system components: the generator, judges (correctness and citation), corrector, and Deep Research system.

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}
\label{sec:methods_system_overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:cld_pipeline} presents the complete CLD generation, judging, and correction pipeline. The system operates in three phases:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Generation:} The LLM generator (GPT-4.1) constructs CLDs through iterative node and edge generation, capturing uncertainty quantification (UQ) metrics for downstream hallucination detection.
    \item \textbf{Judging:} An LLM judge evaluates each edge using either correctness-based reasoning assessment or citation-based verification, producing per-edge verdicts.
    \item \textbf{Correction:} An LLM corrector applies tool-based fixes to flagged edges, followed by re-judging to validate corrections.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Generator}
\label{sec:generator}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Input:} Domain specification (target phenomenon, spatial/temporal scale, scope), optional seed node set.

\textbf{Process:} The Generator constructs CLDs through iterative node and edge generation (see Appendix~\ref{alg:cld_generation} for algorithmic details):
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Node generation:} Proposes system variables relevant to the domain
    \item \textbf{Edge generation:} For each variable pair, proposes relationship type (\texttt{POSITIVE}, \texttt{NEGATIVE}, \texttt{NONE}) with causal narrative
    \item \textbf{UQ capture:} Records token-level logprobs during generation for downstream uncertainty quantification
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Output:} Complete CLD (nodes + edges with causal narratives) and per-edge UQ metrics.

\textbf{Note:} In all RQ1 and RQ2 experiments, nodes were seeded with the validation CLD node sets; node generation was disabled to isolate edge-level performance.

\textbf{Configuration:} GPT-4.1, Nitai\_C prompt, T=0.7---selected via preliminary studies (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_studies}).

\subsubsection{UQ Metrics Capture}
\label{sec:uq_metrics_definition}

UQ metrics are computed solely from the generator LLM's internal signals during edge generation---they do not require external context. We capture four UQ metrics from generator logprobs and embeddings (see Appendix~\ref{alg:uq_metrics} for algorithmic details):

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Expected} \\
\midrule
Perplexity & $\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} -\log P(x_i)\right)$ & $\uparrow \Rightarrow$ halluc \\[1.5ex]
Min Probability & $\displaystyle\min_i P(x_i)$ & $\downarrow \Rightarrow$ halluc \\[1.5ex]
Max Window Entropy & $\displaystyle\max_w \frac{1}{|w|} \sum_{t \in w} H_k(t)$ & $\uparrow \Rightarrow$ halluc \\[1.5ex]
Cosine Similarity & $\displaystyle\max_c \frac{\mathbf{e}_{narr} \cdot \mathbf{e}_c}{\|\mathbf{e}_{narr}\| \|\mathbf{e}_c\|}$ & $\downarrow \Rightarrow$ halluc \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Where: $P(x_i) = \exp(\text{logprob}_i)$, $H_k(t)$ = entropy over top-$k$ tokens, $\mathbf{e}$ = embeddings (mpnet, $d=768$).

\subsection{Correctness Judge}
\label{sec:correctness_judge}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Input:} Variables, relationship type, causal narrative, prompt type.

\textbf{Process:} LLM-as-a-judge~\cite{zheng2024llmasajudge} assesses edge correctness by verifying logical/scientific coherence of the causal claim (see Appendix~\ref{alg:judge_correctness} for algorithmic details).

\textbf{Output Scores:} 0=Incorrect, 0.5=Partially correct, 1=Correct.

\textbf{Configuration:} GPT-4.1, T=0 for reproducibility~\citep{liu2023gevalnlgevaluationusing, zheng2024judging}.

\subsubsection{Prompt Variants}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Baseline} (1500 max output tokens): Direct evaluation without scaffolding
    \item \textbf{Mechanistic} (1500 max output tokens): Structured multi-criteria evaluation using Bradford Hill-aligned framework~\cite{shimonovich2020bradford} (Plausibility, Temporality, Strength, Coherence)
    \item \textbf{Chain-of-Thought} (10000 max output tokens): Explicit step-by-step reasoning~\cite{kojima2022large} with systematic evaluation of causal logic
\end{enumerate}
Full prompt texts are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:correctness_prompts}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Citation Judge}
\label{sec:citation_judge}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Input:} Variables, relationship type, causal narrative, prompt type.

\textbf{Process:} LLM-as-a-judge~\cite{zheng2024llmasajudge} verifies if Brave-fetched articles (up to 5) support the causal narrative (see Appendix~\ref{alg:judge_citation} for algorithmic details).

\textbf{Output Scores:} 0=Not supported, 0.5=Partially supported, 1=Fully supported.

\textbf{Configuration:} GPT-4.1 (or GPT-5-mini for cost reduction in GT Synth), T=0 for reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Prompt Variants}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Baseline} (1500 max output tokens): Direct evaluation without scaffolding
    \item \textbf{Mechanistic} (1500 max output tokens): Bradford Hill-aligned framework
    \item \textbf{Chain-of-Thought} (10000 max output tokens): Explicit step-by-step reasoning
    \item \textbf{Mechanistic-Lit} (1500 max output tokens): Mechanistic prompt enhanced with explicit literature grounding requirements
\end{enumerate}
Full prompt texts are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:citation_prompts}.

\subsubsection{Retrieval Mechanism}

Citation retrieval uses a two-tier fetcher: Jina AI Reader (primary, paywall-bypassing) with ContentScraper fallback (BeautifulSoup-based). See Appendix~\ref{alg:citation_fetcher} for the full fetcher pipeline including retries/timeouts and PDF fallback.

\subsubsection{Retrieval Strategy: Generate-then-Search}
\label{sec:retrieval_strategy}

The Citation Judge follows a \textbf{generate-then-search} paradigm: the generator produces edges without retrieval constraints, and the judge subsequently searches for supporting literature. This decouples generation recall from retrieval coverage---the generator leverages its full parametric knowledge, while retrieval serves as post-hoc validation rather than a constraint on generation. See Background Section~3.6.3 for motivation and limitations of this approach.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Corrector}
\label{sec:corrector}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Input:} Variables, relationship type, causal narrative, judgement from LLM-as-a-judge.

\textbf{Process:} Changes edge by (see Appendix~\ref{alg:corrector} for algorithmic details):
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{a) Changing relationship type:} e.g., \texttt{None}$\rightarrow$\texttt{Positive}, \texttt{Negative}$\rightarrow$\texttt{None}
    \item \textbf{b) Revising motivation:} Rewriting causal narrative with improved reasoning/evidence
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Configuration:} GPT-4.1, T=0.7 for exploration~\citep{madaan2023selfrefineiterativerefinementselffeedback}. Full prompt variants (Baseline, CoT, Mechanistic) are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:corrector_prompts}.

\subsubsection{Direction Correction Mechanism}
Because all pairs are evaluated bidirectionally ($A \rightarrow B$ and $B \rightarrow A$), direction errors are corrected via type changes:

\textbf{Example:} If $A \rightarrow B$ is erroneous (should be $B \rightarrow A$):
$A \xrightarrow{\texttt{NONE}} B$ (remove wrong) + $B \xrightarrow{\texttt{POS}} A$ (add correct)

\subsection{Deep Research System}
\label{sec:deep_research_system}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Deep Research (DR) system is a multi-agent pipeline that iteratively searches scientific literature for evidence supporting or refuting causal relationships.

\subsubsection{Multi-Agent Architecture}

The system is implemented using the PydanticAI framework~\cite{pydanticai2024} with typed agent definitions and structured outputs. Seven specialized LLM agents are coordinated by a Python orchestrator class in a multi-iteration pipeline (see Appendix~\ref{alg:deep_research} for algorithmic details). The full multi-agent pipeline is documented in Table~\ref{tab:dr_agents} in Appendix~\ref{sec:dr_agents_appendix}.

\textbf{Models:} The Deep Research system uses a two-tier model architecture:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Heavy model (GPT-5):} Used for complex reasoning tasks---hypothesis evaluation, article selection, causal evidence detection, judgment, and verdict synthesis. Temperature = 0.3.
    \item \textbf{Light model (GPT-5-mini):} Used for high-volume, lower-complexity tasks---iterative web search and evidence scoring. Temperature = 0.3.
\end{itemize}

\input{figures/deep_research_flowchart}

\subsubsection{Pipeline Execution Flow}

The research process follows an 8-step pipeline with parallel execution where possible:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Search Plan Generation:} The \texttt{hypothesis\_evaluator} generates 3--4 search queries based on the causal claim, plus the verbatim motivation as Query \#1.
    \item \textbf{Parallel Search Execution:} All queries execute in parallel via \texttt{asyncio.gather()}. The \texttt{search\_agent} makes 4--6 iterative \texttt{web\_search} tool calls per query, with a hard limit of 5 searches per thread.
    \item \textbf{Evidence Scoring (Parallel):} All retrieved evidence is scored for relevance (1--10) and quality (1--10) based on study design.
    \item \textbf{Smart Article Selection:} The \texttt{article\_selector} identifies 2--3 articles most likely to contain causal evidence.
    \item \textbf{Full-Text Fetching:} Selected articles are fetched using BeautifulSoup HTML parsing (max 50K characters).
    \item \textbf{Direct Causal Evidence Detection:} The \texttt{causal\_evidence\_detector} evaluates with strict criteria---only RCTs, experimental manipulations, natural experiments, and meta-analyses qualify.
    \item \textbf{Judgment:} The \texttt{judgment\_agent} assesses whether accumulated evidence supports the claim.
    \item \textbf{Final Verdict Synthesis:} The \texttt{verdict\_agent} integrates all evidence into a final verdict.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Search Infrastructure}

\textbf{Search Provider:} Brave Search API with academic domain filtering (23 trusted domains including PubMed, arXiv, Nature, NEJM, Cochrane Library).

\textbf{Query Enhancement:} All queries are automatically appended with ``scientific study research evidence'' to bias toward academic content.

\textbf{Retry Strategy:} 3 retries with exponential backoff for transient failures. Timeouts: 10s connect, 40s read.

\subsubsection{Early Stopping Criteria}

The system terminates early when any of the following conditions are met:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Strong evidence found:} Direct causal evidence detected AND $\geq$3 high-quality sources (relevance $\geq 8$, quality $\geq 7$)
    \item \textbf{High confidence support:} Judgment supported with confidence $\geq 8$
    \item \textbf{Time limit:} 120 seconds elapsed
    \item \textbf{Max iterations:} Configured iteration limit reached
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Data}
\label{sec:experimental_data}
%==============================================================================

This section documents the CLD domains, context parameters, and validation datasets used across all experiments.

\subsection{CLD Domains}
Three domain-specific Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs) representing distinct scientific disciplines:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Depressive Symptoms~\cite{uleman2024triangulation}:} Biopsychosocial model (14 variables, 34 edges) capturing feedback loops between cognitive, behavioral, and physiological factors in depressive states. Constructed via triangulation of group model building, literature review, and causal discovery.
    \item \textbf{Social Norms \& Obesity~\cite{crielaard2020socialnorms}:} Public health model (10 variables, 12 edges) representing interplay between social norms, body weight perception, and behavioral dynamics. Developed using system dynamics modeling with the HELIUS cohort.
    \item \textbf{Older Persons Emergency Department~\cite{smeekes2023emergency}:} Healthcare systems model (34 variables, 66 edges) of factors influencing older persons' ED utilization patterns. Constructed through group model building with interdisciplinary clinical experts.
\end{enumerate}
The exact ground-truth CLD datasets (including filenames and locations) are listed in Appendix~\ref{sec:data_availability} (Table~\ref{tab:gt_files}).

\textbf{Note on ``edges'' and validation edges used in evaluation.} Throughout Results, an \textbf{edge} is defined as an ordered (source, target) \textit{edge slot} with a relationship label in \{\texttt{POSITIVE}, \texttt{NEGATIVE}, \texttt{NONE}\}, i.e., the full directed pair set with \textit{Edges} $=|V|(|V|-1)$ represented by the \texttt{All Edges} table. The effective number of ground-truth validation edges used for scoring is the count of rows with \texttt{In Validation Graph = Yes} (so \textit{TP}+\textit{FN} equals the validation-edge count under this representation).
\textbf{Note on node generation vs.\ edge-only evaluation.} The system supports both node generation and edge generation (Appendix~\ref{alg:cld_generation}) and we evaluate node generation prompt robustness in Appendix~\ref{sec:ablation_node}; however, in the edge-level experiments (RQ1a/RQ1b/RQ2/RQ3) we \textbf{fix the node set to the validation CLD's ground-truth variables} and generate \textbf{edges only} to isolate edge discovery and downstream validation.

\textbf{Rationale:} Following system dynamics principles~\cite{sterman2002system}, variables acting on smaller temporal scales than the variable of interest can be averaged out, while variables on larger scales can be treated as constants. By specifying explicit temporal boundaries, the LLM generator is guided to focus on causal mechanisms operating within the relevant timeframe---e.g., acute stressor responses (weeks--months) for depressive symptoms versus monthly social dynamics for obesity prevalence. Similarly, spatial scale constrains whether causal relationships operate at the individual, community, or healthcare system level. The scope parameter further constrains the domain of variables the LLM should consider.

\subsection{CLD Selection Rationale}
Ground truth CLDs were selected from peer-reviewed publications with explicitly defined causal structures (named variables, directed edges, polarities). The three CLDs span distinct scientific domains---biopsychosocial health, public health behavior, and healthcare systems---reducing the risk of domain-specific overfitting. CLD sizes range from 12 to 66 edges (validation sets: Social Norms 12, Depressive 34, Older Persons ED 66), providing variation in system complexity.

\textbf{Limitation:} All three CLDs fall within the health domain, limiting generalizability to other fields (e.g., economics, ecology, engineering). Additionally, these CLDs represent expert-hypothesized relationships rather than empirically validated causal effects---a common limitation in complex systems where controlled experiments are infeasible~\cite{uleman2024triangulation,crielaard2020socialnorms,smeekes2023emergency}. Evaluation therefore measures reproduction of published expert models, which may or may not be causally correct.

\subsection{Edge Structure}
Each CLD edge contains: \textbf{source/target variables} (named entities with definitions), \textbf{relationship polarity} (\texttt{POSITIVE}/\texttt{NEGATIVE}/\texttt{NONE}), \textbf{causal motivation} (natural language mechanism explanation), and \textbf{scientific citations} (peer-reviewed references).
The standardized output artifacts produced per run (including judged Excel files, JSON metrics, and naming conventions) are documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:output_artifacts} (Table~\ref{tab:output_files}).

\end{minipage}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{Experimental Design Rationale}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{GT Lit experiments:} CLD generation varies (seeds 10, 20, 30) $\rightarrow$ tests judge generalization across CLD instances
    \item \textbf{GT Synth experiments:} Corruption varies (seeds 1, 2, 3), base CLD fixed $\rightarrow$ isolates corruption detection ability
    \item \textbf{Seed separation:} Methodological independence between paradigms (10/20/30 vs 1/2/3)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Confound Prevention:} The seed structure explicitly controls for potential confounds. In GT Synth experiments, fixing the base CLD while varying only the corruption seed ensures that performance differences reflect the judge's ability to detect different corruption patterns---not differences in underlying CLD structure. In GT Lit experiments, CLD structure variation is intentional: we deliberately test whether the judge generalizes across different valid CLD realizations. The seed separation (10/20/30 for generation vs. 1/2/3 for corruption) maintains methodological independence.

\subsubsection{Pipeline Components (Summary)}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The experimental pipeline consists of four core components (see Section~\ref{sec:system_architecture} for full details):

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Generator (GPT-4.1, T=0.7):} Generates CLDs by iteratively proposing nodes and edges with causal narratives. Captures UQ metrics (perplexity, min probability, max window entropy) during generation for RQ2.
    \item \textbf{Judge (GPT-4.1/GPT-5-mini, T=0.0):} Evaluates each edge via correctness-based reasoning or citation-based literature verification. Outputs scores: 0 (reject), 0.5 (uncertain), 1 (accept).
    \item \textbf{Corrector (GPT-4.1, T=0.7):} Attempts to repair judge-flagged hallucinations by revising causal narratives or changing edge types.
    \item \textbf{Deep Research (GPT-5, T=0.3):} Multi-agent literature search pipeline that finds direct causal evidence for edge claims (RQ3 only).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Definitions and Output Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics_definitions}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section consolidates all metrics used across experiments.

\paragraph{Classification Metrics (Judge Evaluation).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{AUC-ROC:} Area under ROC curve (judge score vs. hallucination status). Baseline: AUC = 0.5 (random classification).
    \item \textbf{Correlation ($r$):} Point-biserial correlation between judge score and hallucination label.
    \item \textbf{Classification} (threshold: judge score $\leq 0.5$ = Hallucination): Precision, Recall, \textbf{F1} = $\frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Judge Output Scores.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Correctness Judge:} 0 (reject), 0.5 (uncertain), 1 (accept)
    \item \textbf{Citation Judge:} 0 (no evidence), 0.5 (partial), 1 (full support)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Correction Metrics (RQ1b).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{$\Delta$F1, $\Delta$Precision, $\Delta$Recall:} Change from pre- to post-correction w.r.t.\ ground truth
    \item \textbf{$\Delta$Judge Score:} $\text{mean}(\text{corrected edges}) - \text{mean}(\text{original edges})$
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{UQ Metrics (RQ2).} Context-insensitive metrics captured during generation (see Section~\ref{sec:uq_metrics_definition} for full definitions):
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Perplexity:} Average token-level uncertainty (capped NLL)
    \item \textbf{Min Probability:} Confidence floor---minimum token probability
    \item \textbf{Max Window Entropy:} Highest-uncertainty region (5-token sliding window)
    \item \textbf{Cosine Similarity:} Semantic alignment between narrative and citations (citation experiments only)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Deep Research Metrics (RQ3).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Detection Rate:} Fraction of edges with \texttt{direct\_causal\_found=True}
    \item \textbf{TP-FP Gap:} $\text{DetectionRate}_{\text{TP}} - \text{DetectionRate}_{\text{FP}}$; $>$20pp indicates meaningful discrimination
    \item \textbf{Confidence Score:} Multi-agent verdict strength (0--1 scale)
\end{itemize}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Block-Level Design}
\label{sec:block_design}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Experiments use a \textbf{blocked (repeated-measures) design} with 9 independent blocks: 3 CLDs $\times$ 3 runs (seeds 10, 20, 30 for generation; 1, 2, 3 for corruption). This structure:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Controls for CLD-level variance:} Performance is confounded by domain difficulty (Depressive vs.\ Obesity vs.\ Social Norms); blocking isolates treatment effects.
    \item \textbf{Controls for run-to-run variance:} Different seeds produce different CLD instances; blocking treats each (CLD, run) pair as a subject.
    \item \textbf{Enables within-subject analysis:} Prompt variants or methods are compared within the same block, increasing statistical power.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Unit of inference:} The block mean (average across prompts within a block) is the unit of analysis for meta-level statistical tests. This yields $N=9$ independent observations for hypothesis testing.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Non-Parametric Testing Framework}
\label{sec:nonparam_framework}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For prompt sensitivity analysis (RQ1a, RQ1b), we use non-parametric tests:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Friedman test:} Non-parametric equivalent of repeated-measures ANOVA; ranks observations within each block and tests whether rank sums differ across treatments.
    \item \textbf{Post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests:} Pairwise comparisons between specific prompts within matched blocks.
    \item \textbf{Effect size:} Kendall's $W$ ($<$0.1 negligible, 0.1--0.3 small, 0.3--0.5 medium, $\geq$0.5 large).
\end{itemize}

This framework is robust to small sample sizes ($n=9$ blocks), non-normality, and outliers.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Multiple Comparison Corrections}
\label{sec:multiple_comparisons}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Bonferroni corrections control family-wise error rate, grouped by research question:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{RQ1a:} 3 pairwise comparisons ($k=3$ prompts) $\rightarrow$ $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0167$; 6 comparisons ($k=4$) $\rightarrow$ $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0083$
    \item \textbf{RQ1b:} 2 omnibus Friedman tests $\rightarrow$ $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.025$; post-hoc 3 pairs $\rightarrow$ $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0167$
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} 4 single-metric tests $\rightarrow$ $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0125$
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} 3 Fisher's exact tests $\rightarrow$ $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.017$
\end{itemize}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Effect Size Reporting}
\label{sec:effect_sizes}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All significant findings include standardized effect sizes (Cohen, 1988):
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Cohen's $f$ (ANOVA):} $<$0.10 negligible, 0.10--0.25 small, 0.25--0.40 medium, $\geq$0.40 large
    \item \textbf{Cohen's $d$ (paired differences):} $<$0.20 negligible, 0.20--0.50 small, 0.50--0.80 medium, $\geq$0.80 large
    \item \textbf{Cohen's $h$ (proportions):} Same thresholds as $d$
    \item \textbf{Pearson $r$:} $<$0.10 negligible, 0.10--0.30 small, 0.30--0.50 medium, $\geq$0.50 large
\end{itemize}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Uncertainty Reporting Rule}
\label{sec:uncertainty_rule}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For repeated measurements (across CLDs, runs/seeds, or CV folds), we report \textbf{mean [min, max]} as an assumption-free summary. 95\% confidence intervals are reserved for instance-level evaluations with large sample sizes ($N \geq 30$) or block-level meta-inference ($N=9$ blocks, t-distribution with $df=8$).

\textbf{Interpretation:} mean [min, max] is descriptive; 95\% CIs are inferential under the stated model. CV fold scores are not independent draws (folds share training data), so fold-based intervals should not be interpreted as strict confidence intervals.

Post-hoc power analysis assessed the probability of detecting observed effects at $\alpha = 0.05$:

\subsubsection{Model Selection}

\subsubsection{Prompt Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:rq1a_sensitivity}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To quantify prompt engineering impact on judge performance, we employ a variance-based sensitivity analysis framework inspired by Sobol indices~\cite{sobol1993sensitivity}. Using a \textbf{blocked design} with CLD$\times$run as subjects and prompt as a within-subject factor, we report \textbf{partial $\eta^2$} as the proportion of within-subject variance explained by prompt choice (see Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity_analysis} for full specification).

\paragraph{Input Quantification.}
We quantify semantic difference between prompt variants by embedding each prompt using MPNet~\cite{song2020mpnet} (768-dim) and computing pairwise cosine distances:
\begin{equation}
d(p_i, p_j) = 1 - \cos(\mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{e}_j)
\end{equation}
The variance $\text{Var}(d)$ characterizes the semantic spread of the prompt set.

\paragraph{Output Variance Decomposition.}
Using repeated-measures ANOVA, we report partial eta-squared:
\begin{equation}
\eta^2_p = \frac{SS_{\text{prompt}}}{SS_{\text{prompt}} + SS_{\text{error}}}
\end{equation}
For a single categorical factor, $\eta^2$ corresponds to the first-order Sobol sensitivity index~\cite{saltelli2010variance}; see Appendix~\ref{app:sobol_eta2_derivation} for derivation.

\paragraph{Normalized Sensitivity.}
To relate input perturbation magnitude to output variance:
\begin{equation}
S_{norm} = \frac{\eta^2_p}{\text{Var}(d)}
\end{equation}
Higher values indicate small semantic changes produce proportionally larger output effects (high sensitivity); low values indicate robustness.

\paragraph{Scope.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Judge (RQ1a):} 4 experiments $\times$ 3--4 prompt variants (Baseline, CoT, Mechanistic, and Mechanistic-Lit for citation)
    \item \textbf{Corrector (RQ1b):} 2 experiments $\times$ 3 prompts (cumulative design)
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Search Providers (4):} OpenAlex (100M+ papers), Brave Search (with/without domain whitelist), Perplexity (AI-powered academic focus), PubMed (35M+ biomedical citations)
    \item \textbf{Citation Fetchers (2):} Jina AI Reader (paywall-bypassing, LLM-optimized extraction), ContentScraper (BeautifulSoup-based); see Appendix~\ref{alg:citation_fetcher} for pipeline details

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Human-LLM Agreement}
\label{sec:human_llm_agreement}

To validate that LLM citation judges produce reliable verdicts, we conducted human expert validation measuring inter-rater agreement.

\textbf{Dataset:} 72 causal edges across 3 validation files, spanning 2 CLDs and 2 generators:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item 30 edges from Depressive symptoms CLD (GPT-4.1)
    \item 30 edges from Depressive symptoms CLD (Claude 4 Sonnet)
    \item 12 edges from Social norms CLD (GPT-4.1)
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Raters:} Human validator (domain expert) provided categorical verdicts: Not supported (0), Partially supported (0.5), or Fully supported (1). LLM judge: Citation-based (GPT-4.1, T=0, baseline prompt).

\textbf{Metrics:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Cohen's $\kappa$:} Chance-corrected agreement; $\kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1 - P_e}$
    \item \textbf{Agreement Rate:} Exact match percentage
    \item \textbf{Pearson $r$:} Score correlation (0, 0.5, 1 scale)
\end{itemize}
Interpretation (Landis \& Koch, 1977): $\kappa < 0.20$ = slight; $0.21$--$0.40$ = fair; $0.41$--$0.60$ = moderate; $0.61$--$0.80$ = substantial; $> 0.80$ = almost perfect.

\textbf{Baseline:} Human inter-rater reliability from prior GMB studies ($\kappa \approx 0.60$, 80\% agreement) establishes the ceiling for automated judge performance.

\textbf{Hypothesis:} If citation-based LLM judges reliably assess evidence quality, we expect: (1) substantial agreement ($\kappa > 0.60$), (2) performance comparable to human baseline, and (3) consistency across CLDs and generators.

\subsubsection{Implementation Choices}

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l l l X@{}}

NLL cap & 20.0 (token), 10.0 (avg) & Design choice & Prevents single outlier tokens ($P < 2 \times 10^{-9}$) from dominating the average; average cap bounds perplexity to a finite maximum ($\leq e^{10} \approx 22{,}026$) to avoid extreme values \\

\textbf{Perplexity computation:} Two-stage capping improves robustness and avoids numerical instability: (1) individual token NLL values are capped at 20.0 before averaging ($\text{nll}_i = \min(-\log P(x_i), 20.0)$), preventing single outlier tokens ($P < 2 \times 10^{-9}$) from dominating; (2) the average NLL is capped at 10.0, bounding perplexity at $e^{10} \approx 22{,}026$ to prevent extreme values from overwhelming comparisons.

\textbf{Min Probability:} The minimum token probability across the entire generated sequence, representing the ``confidence floor''---the single least-confident token prediction. Low minimum probability indicates at least one token was generated with high uncertainty, which we treat as a candidate indicator of localized uncertainty. This metric is complementary to perplexity: while perplexity captures average uncertainty, min probability captures worst-case uncertainty.

\textbf{Max Window Entropy:} Two-step aggregation detects localized uncertainty spikes: (1) at each token position, compute entropy over top-$k=5$ alternatives returned by the API~\cite{openai2024api}: $H_k(t) = -\sum_{j=1}^{k} \tilde{p}_j \log \tilde{p}_j$ where $\tilde{p}_j = \text{softmax}(\text{logprob}_j)$; (2) apply a sliding window of size 5 to compute rolling averages via \texttt{np.convolve}, then return the maximum: $H_{\text{max-win}} = \max_i \frac{1}{w}\sum_{t=i}^{i+w-1} H_k(t)$. This identifies the highest-uncertainty region in the generated text.

\textbf{Cosine Similarity:} Embeddings are computed locally using \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2}~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} (768-dim, 384 token limit). Citation texts are chunked with adaptive window: chunk size $= \max(256, \min(4000, |\text{motiv.}|))$ characters, 20\% overlap, de-duplicated. This adaptive sizing ensures chunks remain within the model's 384-token context (motivations average 56 tokens, max 137; implied chunks 57--176 tokens). For each edge, $\text{sim} = \max_c \cos(\mathbf{e}_{\text{narr}}, \mathbf{e}_c)$ over all citation chunks. Available only for citation experiments.

\textbf{Model Selection:} We selected \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} based on benchmarking against Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Appendix~\ref{sec:embedding_benchmark}): 22$\times$ faster (3.0 vs.\ 67.3~ms/doc) with superior MTEB reranking performance (59.36 vs.\ 51.56)~\cite{muennighoff2023mteb}.

\subsubsection{Hallucination Definition}
$\text{Hallucination} = (\text{Classification} = \text{FP}) \lor (\text{Classification} = \text{FN})$ w.r.t. GT Lit

\subsubsection{Analysis Pipeline (6 Phases)}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Phase 1-3:} Single-metric analysis. Compute per-file AUC-ROC and correlations, then aggregate to independent blocks (CLD$\times$run, $N=9$) for inferential testing of above-chance discrimination (one-sample t-test and t-based 95\% CI on block-mean AUCs). For distribution diagnostics, compute within-block rank-based separation (Mann--Whitney U and rank-biserial $r$ within each block) and summarize these effect sizes across blocks (no edge-level inferential claims).
    \item \textbf{Phase 4 (RFE):} Recursive Feature Elimination~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn} with block-level 3-fold GroupKFold CV. Blocks = (CLD$\times$run, $N=9$); entire blocks stay together in train or test to prevent pseudo-replication. Each classifier performs independent feature selection; best classifier (by CV AUC) determines features for Phase 5--6. Classifiers: LogReg, RF, GradBoost (Neural Network excluded---incompatible with RFE). \textit{Output:} Mean CV AUC across 3 folds per classifier; best classifier's selected features passed downstream.
    \item \textbf{Phase 5 (Ensemble):} Uses features from the best Phase 4 classifier on a block-level train/test split via GroupShuffleSplit ($\sim$67\% train blocks, $\sim$33\% test blocks). Training blocks: 3-fold GroupKFold CV for model comparison. Test blocks: held-out final evaluation. Classifiers: all 4 (adds Neural Network). \textit{Output:} CV AUC (on training blocks) + Test AUC (on held-out blocks). This block-level splitting ensures entire (CLD$\times$run) generation scenarios remain together, preventing leakage from correlated edges within the same scenario.
    \item \textbf{Phase 6 (Leave-One-CLD-Out):} Cross-domain generalization via leave-one-CLD-out CV. Train on 2 CLDs, test on held-out 3rd CLD; repeat for each of 3 CLDs. \textit{Output:} Mean AUC across 3 held-out CLD tests.
\end{enumerate}
The end-to-end classifier training/evaluation procedure is specified formally in Appendix~\ref{alg:classification}.

\paragraph{Reproducible Splitting Protocol (RQ2, Phases 4--6).}
To maximize reproducibility and avoid leakage, we fully specify the data splitting and preprocessing pipeline:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Grouping key (block definition):} Each edge is assigned to a generation scenario block $\texttt{block\_id} = \texttt{cld} \,\|\, \texttt{\_} \,\|\, \texttt{run}$ (3 CLDs $\times$ 3 runs $\Rightarrow N=9$ blocks). All group-aware splits operate on these blocks. Within each block, multiple prompt variants are treated as repeated conditions (not independent samples).
    \item \textbf{Deterministic fold composition:} Prior to any group split, rows are sorted deterministically by $(\texttt{block\_id}, \texttt{source\_file})$ so that GroupKFold fold assignments are stable across runs.
    \item \textbf{Phase 4 (RFE CV):} \texttt{GroupKFold(n\_splits=3)} over blocks. \textbf{What each fold contains:} each fold holds out \textbf{3 entire blocks} (validation) and trains on the remaining \textbf{6 blocks}; all edges from the held-out blocks constitute that fold's validation set. No stratification is applied at the edge level (because grouping is prioritized to prevent pseudo-replication). Within each fold, \textbf{StandardScaler is fit on the fold's training blocks only} and applied to the fold's held-out blocks. RFE evaluates all $k\\in\\{1,2,3,4\\}$ feature subset sizes and selects the $k$ maximizing validation AUC within that fold; selection stability is reported as frequency across the 3 folds.
    \item \textbf{Phase 5 (train/test + CV):} A single block-level split is sampled using \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit(n\_splits=1, test\_size=0.33, random\_state=42)}; \textbf{what the split contains:} \textbf{3 blocks} are held out as the Phase-5 test set and the remaining \textbf{6 blocks} form the Phase-5 training set (all edges from those blocks). Model selection on the training blocks uses \texttt{GroupKFold(n\_splits=3)}; \textbf{what each Phase-5 CV fold contains:} each fold holds out \textbf{2 blocks} (validation) and trains on \textbf{4 blocks}. \textbf{Scaling is always fit on training data only:} a global scaler is fit on Phase-5 training blocks and applied to the held-out Phase-5 test blocks; for Phase-5 CV, scaling is fit within each CV-training fold and applied to the corresponding CV-validation fold.
    \item \textbf{Phase 6 (LOCO):} Leave-one-CLD-out: \textbf{what each fold contains:} all edges from one entire CLD constitute the test fold, and all edges from the other two CLDs constitute the training fold. Preprocessing (scaling) is fit on the 2 training CLDs and applied to the held-out CLD. Uncertainty is reported as mean [min, max] across the 3 held-out CLDs.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
UQ metrics not applicable for GT Synth (corruption post-generation, logprobs not captured). Cosine similarity only for Citation Judge (requires fetched embeddings).

\subsubsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:rq2_analysis}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To account for the hierarchical data structure---where edges are nested within CLDs and prompts share the same generation run---we adopt a \textbf{block-level aggregation} strategy, treating the generation run block (CLD $\times$ Run, $N=9$) as the independent unit of analysis.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Unit of Analysis:} Inference is performed on $N=9$ independent blocks (3 CLDs $\times$ 3 runs). Within each block, metric performance (AUC, correlation) is averaged across prompts to marginalize over prompt-specific variation.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis Testing:} We test whether the mean block-level AUC exceeds chance (0.5) using a \textbf{one-sample t-test} on the 9 block means. While individual edge-level metrics are non-normal (justifying Mann-Whitney U for raw comparisons), the block-level means follow a normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk $p=0.67$), satisfying assumptions for parametric inference.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty Quantification:} 95\% confidence intervals for the mean AUC are computed via the \textbf{t-distribution} ($df=8$), ensuring consistency between CIs and hypothesis tests.
    \item \textbf{Correlation Aggregation:} Pearson correlations ($r$) are aggregated across blocks using the \textbf{Fisher z-transformation} to stabilize variance, then transformed back to the correlation scale.
    \item \textbf{Distributional Comparisons:} For visualizing separation between hallucinated and correct edges, we employ the Mann-Whitney U test on pooled edge distributions per CLD, acknowledging that this treats edges as the unit of description rather than inference.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Metrics}

\textbf{Detection Rate:} The primary metric is the percentage of edges where \texttt{direct\_causal\_found = True}:
\[
\text{Detection Rate} = \frac{|\{e : \texttt{direct\_causal\_found}(e) = \texttt{True}\}|}{|E|} \times 100\%
\]
This measures the system's ability to find \textit{causal} (not just correlational) evidence in the scientific literature.

\textbf{TP-FP Discrimination Gap:}
\[
\Delta_{\text{TP-FP}} = \text{DetectionRate}_{\text{TP}} - \text{DetectionRate}_{\text{FP}}
\]
A positive gap indicates the system can distinguish expert-accepted edges from LLM hallucinations. We consider $\Delta > 20$ percentage points as meaningful discrimination.

\textbf{Confidence Scores:} Mean self-assessed confidence (1--10) by classification. Tests whether the system can reliably self-assess the validity of its findings.

\textbf{Hypothesis (H6):} If Deep Research can validate causal claims, we expect: (1) high detection rate for TP (expert-accepted edges should have literature support), (2) low detection rate for FP (hallucinations should lack support), and (3) significant TP-FP gap ($>$20 percentage points).

\subsubsection{Analysis}

We test whether Deep Research discriminates between classes using pairwise two-sample proportion comparisons on the binary outcome \texttt{direct\_causal\_found}.

\textbf{Aggregate discrimination tests (pooled across CLDs).} We construct 2$\times$2 contingency tables of \textit{Found} vs.\ \textit{Not Found} for each pairwise comparison among TP, FP, and FN. For each pair, we report:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Fisher's exact test} (primary; always valid for $2\times 2$ tables)
    \item \textbf{Chi-square test} with Cochran's rule assumption checks
    \item \textbf{Cohen's $h$} for proportion differences: $h = 2\arcsin(\sqrt{p_1}) - 2\arcsin(\sqrt{p_2})$, with 95\% CI via delta-method
    \item \textbf{Odds ratio} with 95\% CI (Woolf method; Haldane--Anscombe correction if any cell is zero)
    \item \textbf{Post-hoc power analysis:} achieved power and minimum detectable effect at 80\% power
\end{itemize}
We treat the three pairwise comparisons (TP--FP, TP--FN, FP--FN) as a single family and apply Bonferroni correction with $m=3$ ($\alpha_{\text{adj}}=0.05/3 \approx 0.017$), reporting both raw and adjusted p-values.

\textbf{Per-CLD discrimination tests.} To quantify domain dependence, we repeat the same three pairwise tests \textit{within each CLD} (Depressive, Social Norms, Older Persons). Within each CLD, we again apply Bonferroni correction over the three within-CLD pairwise tests ($m=3$) and report raw and adjusted p-values and Cohen's $h$.

\textbf{Implementation reference (reproducible).} Both the aggregate test table and the per-CLD pairwise table are generated by \texttt{python final\_runs/RQ3\_deep\_research/run\_rq3\_analysis.py}, which computes Fisher's exact tests, Cohen's $h$ (via the arcsine transform), and Bonferroni-adjusted p-values consistent with the Results tables (\texttt{rq3\_main\_table.tex} and \texttt{rq3\_per\_cld\_table.tex}).

\subsubsection{Exploratory Analysis: Ground Truth Enrichment}
\label{sec:gt_enrichment_method}

Beyond detection rate analysis, we explore whether Deep Research results could \textit{enrich} the expert-constructed ground truth. The hypothesis is that some FP edges (``hallucinations'') represent valid causal relationships that experts omitted due to time constraints, scope decisions, or disciplinary blind spots---not because they lack scientific support.

We evaluate two enrichment scenarios:

\textbf{Scenario 1: Enriched Ground Truth (FP Promotion)}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Identify DR-validated FPs:} FP edges where \texttt{direct\_causal\_found = True}
    \item \textbf{Promote to TP:} Reclassify these edges as True Positives in an ``enriched ground truth''
    \item \textbf{Recompute metrics:} Calculate Precision, Recall, and F1 against the enriched GT
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Question:} ``What if experts missed valid literature-supported relationships?''

\textbf{Scenario 2: LLM+DR System (FP Promotion + FN Discovery)}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Start with Scenario 1:} Enriched GT with promoted FPs
    \item \textbf{Identify DR-validated FNs:} FN edges where \texttt{direct\_causal\_found = True}
    \item \textbf{Add to system output:} In a complete LLM+DR pipeline, DR would search comprehensively and discover these literature-supported edges the LLM missed
    \item \textbf{Recompute metrics:} TP includes discovered FNs; FN decreases accordingly
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Question:} ``What could a complete LLM+DR system achieve if DR both validated LLM output AND discovered additional literature-supported edges?''

\textbf{Rationale:} Scenario 1 tests whether expert GT underestimates LLM quality. Scenario 2 tests the theoretical ceiling of an integrated LLM+DR pipeline where literature search augments generation.

\textbf{Critical Caveats (see Discussion for details):}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Domain variation:} FP promotion rates vary substantially across CLDs (28\% Depressive vs 76\% Older Persons), suggesting inconsistent enrichment
    \item \textbf{Evidence specificity/applicability:} DR may retrieve causal evidence that is only partially aligned to the exact edge claim (proxy variables, different operationalizations, or partial mechanisms); FP promotion should therefore be interpreted only after applicability thresholding and expert review
    \item \textbf{Circularity risk:} LLM-based DR validating LLM-generated edges could introduce systematic bias
    \item \textbf{No expert review:} Enrichment is automated; promoted edges require domain expert validation
    \item \textbf{Preliminary status:} Results are exploratory pending open-source dataset release for community validation
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Human Validation Sampling}
\label{sec:rq3_human_validation}

To validate the Deep Research verdicts, we performed stratified random sampling of edges for human review. From the 285 edges across all three CLDs, we first filtered to include only edges where direct causal evidence was identified (\texttt{direct\_causal\_found=True}), yielding 169 eligible edges. We then applied proportional stratified sampling across two dimensions: (1) CLD and (2) classification type (TP, FP, FN). Sample sizes for each stratum were calculated proportionally to their population size, with a minimum of one edge per stratum to ensure representation of all CLD-classification combinations. The target sample size was 50 edges (29.6\% of eligible edges). This resulted in 10 edges from the Depressive Symptoms CLD (29.4\%), 3 from Social Norms (27.3\%), and 37 from Older Persons (29.8\%), reflecting the underlying distribution. By classification, we sampled 9 TP edges (29.0\%), 32 FP edges (28.8\%), and 9 FN edges (33.3\%). Within each stratum, edges were selected using Python's \texttt{random.sample()} function with a fixed seed (42) for reproducibility. The final sample was then shuffled to randomize presentation order and prevent reviewer bias from sequential patterns.

\textbf{Reproduction:} \texttt{python final\_runs/RQ3\_deep\_research/scripts/sample\_edges\_for\_validation.py}

\vspace{0.25em}
\textbf{Human applicability rubric.} In addition to a categorical verdict (\texttt{causal}, \texttt{different\_vars, causal}, \texttt{invalid}), each sampled edge was assigned a continuous evidence applicability score \texttt{Fully applicable} \(\in[0,1]\), capturing how well the retrieved evidence matches the \textit{specific} (source, target, mechanism) claim rather than merely topical overlap. Scores were assigned using a coarse rubric: \(1.0=\) exact match; \(0.8\text{--}0.9=\) causal match but with different operationalizations/measures; \(\approx 0.7=\) partial claim match (e.g., evidence supports \(A\rightarrow B\) but the edge asserts \(A\rightarrow(B \wedge C)\)); \(\approx 0.6=\) analogous/proxy constructs; \(\le 0.5=\) weak/non-causal study design or otherwise insufficient evidence quality. This rubric is used in Results to choose an applicability threshold and to extrapolate from the validated sample to the full DR dataset.

\subsubsection{Threshold Selection and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:rq3_threshold_selection}

To determine an applicability threshold for filtering DR verdicts, we perform sensitivity analysis across threshold values $t \in \{0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9\}$.

\textbf{Sensitivity metrics.} For each threshold $t$, we compute on the validated sample:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Coverage (pass rate):} Fraction of edges with applicability $\geq t$
    \item \textbf{Correct-causal rate:} Fraction of passing edges labeled \texttt{causal} by human reviewer
    \item \textbf{95\% Wilson CI:} Confidence interval for the correct-causal rate
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Threshold selection rule (Elbow/Gradient).} We select the threshold using a gradient-based elbow rule on the coverage--correctness curve:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item Construct the trade-off curve: $x(t) = \text{coverage}$, $y(t) = \text{correct-causal rate}$
    \item For each adjacent threshold pair moving from stricter to looser (e.g., $0.8 \rightarrow 0.7$), compute:
    \[
    \text{score} = \frac{\Delta \text{coverage}}{|\Delta \text{correctness}|}
    \]
    \item Select the threshold at the \textit{end} of the segment with maximum score (the ``elbow'' point where coverage gains diminish relative to correctness losses)
\end{enumerate}

This rule selects a threshold intended to balance including more edges (higher coverage) against maintaining evidence quality (higher correct-causal rate).

\subsubsection{Extrapolation from Validation Sample}
\label{sec:rq3_extrapolation}

To estimate enrichment potential for the full DR dataset from the 50-edge validated sample, we apply a two-stage extrapolation:

\textbf{Stage 1: Sample statistics.} From the validated sample at the selected threshold $t^*$:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Compute the pass rate: fraction of validated edges with applicability $\geq t^*$
    \item Compute the correct-causal rate among passing edges (with Wilson 95\% CI)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Stage 2: Full dataset projection.} For the full DR dataset ($n=285$ edges):
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Count edges where \texttt{direct\_causal\_found=True} per classification (FP, FN)
    \item Estimate edges above threshold: $\hat{n}_{\text{above}} = n_{\text{direct}} \times \text{pass\_rate}_{\text{sample}}$
    \item Estimate truly causal edges: $\hat{n}_{\text{causal}} = \hat{n}_{\text{above}} \times \text{causal\_rate}_{\text{sample}}$
    \item Conservative estimate uses the lower bound of the 95\% CI for causal rate
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Enrichment scenarios.} We compute projected F1 under two scenarios:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Scenario 1 (Enriched GT):} Promote estimated FP edges to TP in the ground truth
    \item \textbf{Scenario 2 (LLM+DR):} Additionally add estimated FN discoveries to system output
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Caveats:} Extrapolation assumes the validated sample is representative of the full dataset. The stratified sampling design ensures proportional representation, but small strata (e.g., Social Norms with $n=3$) have high sampling variance. Results are reported with point estimates and conservative (lower CI) bounds to reflect this uncertainty.

\textbf{Reproduction:} \texttt{python final\_runs/RQ3\_deep\_research/scripts/enrichment\_extrapolation.py}
