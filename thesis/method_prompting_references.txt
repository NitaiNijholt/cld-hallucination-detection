THESIS METHODS SECTION: PROMPTING STRATEGIES FOR LLM-AS-JUDGE
================================================================================

EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN OVERVIEW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We evaluated three prompting strategies for LLM-as-judge in evaluating causal 
explanations within Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs). This design allows us to test 
whether (1) generic reasoning decomposition improves judgment accuracy, and (2) 
domain-specific adaptation provides additional benefits for causal evaluation tasks.

The three prompting strategies were applied to two evaluation tasks:
  - Correctness Judgment: Assessing the logical soundness of causal explanations
  - Citation Verification: Evaluating whether citations support causal claims


PROMPTING STRATEGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. ZERO-SHOT BASELINE
   Description: Direct judgment without reasoning scaffolding or explicit criteria
   
   Implementation: Minimal prompt asking the LLM to evaluate causal reasoning 
   quality or citation support without providing explicit evaluation steps or 
   criteria.
   
   Theoretical grounding: Establishes baseline LLM performance on causal 
   evaluation tasks without interventions.
   
   Citation: N/A (baseline condition)


2. ZERO-SHOT CHAIN-OF-THOUGHT (CoT)
   Description: Generic reasoning decomposition via the "Let's think step by 
   step" prompt addition
   
   Implementation: Implements Kojima et al.'s (2022) Zero-Shot CoT approach by 
   adding "Let's think step by step" immediately before requesting the judgment. 
   This simple phrase triggers step-by-step reasoning without requiring 
   hand-crafted exemplars or explicit criteria.
   
   Theoretical grounding: Kojima et al. (2022) demonstrated that this single 
   prompt addition significantly improves reasoning performance across diverse 
   tasks, including:
     - MultiArith: 17.7% → 78.7% accuracy
     - GSM8K: 10.4% → 40.7% accuracy
     - Improvements on symbolic reasoning, date understanding, and logical tasks
   
   The authors argue this reveals "untapped and understudied fundamental zero-shot 
   capabilities of LLMs" and suggests "high-level, multi-task broad cognitive 
   capabilities may be extracted by simple prompting."
   
   Citation:
   Kojima, T., Gu, S. S., Reid, M., Matsuo, Y., & Iwasawa, Y. (2022). 
   Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners. In Advances in Neural 
   Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2022). arXiv:2205.11916


3. MECHANISTIC CHAIN-OF-THOUGHT (Bradford Hill Framework)
   Description: Domain-adapted prompting using four Bradford Hill criteria 
   identified as having strong consensus across modern causal inference approaches
   
   Implementation: Prompts explicitly guide evaluation using four criteria:
   
     (1) STRENGTH OF ASSOCIATION (including confounding analysis)
         - Effect sizes and association strengths
         - Discussion of potential confounding variables
         - Evidence that relationships persist after controlling for confounders
         - Consideration of alternative explanations
     
     (2) TEMPORALITY
         - Temporal ordering (cause precedes effect)
         - Ruling out reverse causation
         - Appropriate time lag between cause and effect
         - Longitudinal evidence of temporal sequence
     
     (3) PLAUSIBILITY (causal mechanisms and mediation)
         - Description of HOW causes produce effects
         - Biological/psychological/social mechanism evidence
         - Identification of mediating variables or pathways
         - Specific and empirically supported mechanisms (not just theoretical)
     
     (4) EXPERIMENT/DESIGN (implications for exchangeability)
         - Experimental or quasi-experimental study designs
         - Designs that enhance causal inference (RCTs, natural experiments)
         - Comparability between exposed and unexposed groups
         - Control for factors affecting exchangeability
   
   Theoretical grounding: Bradford Hill (1965) proposed nine viewpoints for 
   assessing causality in epidemiology. Shimonovich et al. (2020) conducted a 
   systematic comparison of Bradford Hill's criteria with modern causal inference 
   approaches (Directed Acyclic Graphs, Sufficient-Component Cause models, and 
   GRADE methodology). They identified four criteria with strong consensus across 
   all approaches:
   
     "Modern comparisons identified commonality on four viewpoints: strength of 
     association (including analysis of plausible confounding); temporality; 
     plausibility (encoded by DAGs or SCC models to articulate mediation and 
     interaction, respectively); and experiments (including implications of study 
     design on exchangeability)." (Shimonovich et al., 2020, p. 873)
   
   These four criteria align with established mechanistic explanation frameworks 
   in philosophy of science:
   
     - Mechanistic plausibility: Machamer, Darden, and Craver (2000) define 
       mechanisms as "entities and activities organized such that they are 
       productive of regular changes from start or set-up to finish or 
       termination conditions," emphasizing that causal explanations must 
       specify HOW causes produce effects.
     
     - Causal direction and intervention: Woodward (2003) argues that "X is a 
       cause of Y if and only if there is a possible intervention on X that 
       changes Y," grounding causality in directional, interventional reasoning.
     
     - Temporality: Universally recognized as fundamental to causality. 
       Shimonovich et al. (2020) note: "Temporality is considered fundamental 
       to causality; an exposure must precede an outcome."
   
   Citations:
   Bradford Hill, A. (1965). The environment and disease: Association or 
   causation? Proceedings of the Royal Society of Medicine, 58(5), 295-300.
   
   Shimonovich, M., Pearce, A., Thomson, H., Keyes, K., & Katikireddi, S. V. 
   (2021). Assessing causality in epidemiology: revisiting Bradford Hill to 
   incorporate developments in causal thinking. European Journal of 
   Epidemiology, 36(9), 873-887. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10654-020-00703-7
   
   Machamer, P., Darden, L., & Craver, C. F. (2000). Thinking about Mechanisms. 
   Philosophy of Science, 67(1), 1-25.
   
   Woodward, J. (2003). Making Things Happen: A Theory of Causal Explanation. 
   Oxford: Oxford University Press.


RESEARCH QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PRIMARY RESEARCH QUESTION:
Does domain-adapted Chain-of-Thought prompting improve LLM-as-judge accuracy 
for evaluating causal explanations in system dynamics models?

SPECIFIC HYPOTHESES:

H1: Chain-of-Thought prompting improves judgment accuracy compared to zero-shot 
    baseline (CoT vs. Baseline)
    
    Expected: Yes, based on CoT literature showing 8-40% improvements on 
    reasoning tasks (Kojima et al., 2022)

H2: Mechanistic focus in CoT further improves accuracy for causal evaluation 
    tasks (Mechanistic CoT vs. generic CoT)
    
    Expected: Yes, based on domain-specific adaptation showing improvements 
    (e.g., CausalCoT achieving 8.37 percentage point improvement over baseline 
    prompting; Jin et al., 2023)

H3: Mechanistic CoT reduces false positives (accepting incorrect causal reasoning)
    
    Expected: Yes, because explicit mechanism checking and temporality 
    verification should catch spurious claims

H4: Improvements generalize across different types of causal relationships 
    (POSITIVE, NEGATIVE, COMPLEX, etc.)
    
    Exploratory: Test if mechanistic focus helps more for complex relationships

H5: Benefits of mechanistic prompting are consistent across both correctness 
    evaluation and citation verification tasks
    
    Expected: Mechanistic focus may provide larger benefits for citation 
    verification, as citations must provide mechanistic evidence to support 
    causal claims


SCIENTIFIC CONTRIBUTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Novel Application Domain:
While prior work has demonstrated Chain-of-Thought's effectiveness for causal 
discovery tasks (Jin et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022), this is the first study 
to systematically evaluate prompting strategies for LLM-as-judge in causal 
explanation evaluation, specifically for system dynamics models.

Methodological Contribution:
This work extends LLM prompting research by:
  1. Applying established prompting techniques (Zero-Shot CoT) to a novel 
     evaluation task (LLM-as-judge for causal reasoning)
  2. Developing and testing domain-adapted prompts grounded in causal inference 
     theory (Bradford Hill criteria)
  3. Comparing generic vs. domain-specific prompting strategies
  4. Testing generalization across two related but distinct evaluation tasks 
     (correctness judgment and citation verification)

Practical Contribution:
Findings directly inform the design of automated evaluation tools for Causal 
Loop Diagram generation, with implications for:
  - Reducing hallucinations in AI-assisted system dynamics modeling
  - Improving quality control for LLM-generated causal explanations
  - Enhancing citation verification in scientific knowledge systems

EXPECTED METRICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Accuracy Metrics:
  - Overall accuracy (agreement with ground truth)
  - False positive rate (Type I error: accepting incorrect explanations)
  - False negative rate (Type II error: rejecting correct explanations)
  - F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall)
  - Cohen's kappa (inter-rater reliability with ground truth)

Consistency Metrics:
  - Inter-trial consistency (same prompt, temperature > 0)
  - Paraphrase robustness (same meaning, different wording)

Efficiency Metrics:
  - Average response length (tokens)
  - Number of tokens generated
  - Inference time
